Floods, droughts and heatwaves cause large damage to water resources and often occur together or in quick succession. Their frequency and severity are controlled by large-scale climate processes, including storm tracks, moisture transport and ocean–atmosphere modes such as the El Niño–Southern Oscillation. A warmer atmosphere holds about 7% more water vapour per degree Celsius and has higher evaporative demand, so warming tends to intensify both wet and dry extremes. Because these controls are changing, risk estimates based only on historical observations can underestimate future extremes. My research examines how large-scale climate processes control extreme precipitation, floods and droughts, and how these extremes change with warming.

My current work focuses on atmospheric rivers, which deliver a large share of California's precipitation and cause many of its largest floods. I examine how the El Niño–Southern Oscillation and the Pacific Decadal Oscillation together influence the frequency and intensity of atmospheric rivers and the extreme precipitation they produce. These climate modes change slowly and can be monitored months in advance, so they provide a physical basis for seasonal outlooks of flood risk. The effects of an atmospheric river also depend on conditions on the ground. Intense rain on recently burned hillslopes can trigger debris flows and carry large amounts of sediment into streams and reservoirs, and rain on wet soils or on snow can increase runoff. Combining atmospheric-river forecasts with information on burn scars, soil moisture and snow could give earlier warning of debris flows, erosion and floods.

My earlier work on floods, droughts and heatwaves in Indian river basins led to a broader question: how extremes interact, and how human systems shape their impact. Extremes rarely act in isolation. Droughts coincide with heatwaves, wet seasons follow dry ones, and reservoir operations and groundwater use change the impact of each extreme. Understanding these interactions, and how they are changing with warming, is central to water security worldwide. My aim is a physically based, global understanding of compound water risks, grounded in detailed studies of individual basins.
